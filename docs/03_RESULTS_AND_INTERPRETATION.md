# TELOS: Results and Interpretation

## Abstract

Predicting regulatory activity from DNA sequence remains challenging when the regulatory landscape changes with cellular state. TELOS evaluates AlphaGenome's predicted ATAC-seq signal against experimentally observed chromatin accessibility in WI-38 fibroblasts undergoing replicative senescence. We constructed a benchmark of 10,000 genomic regions comprising senescence-associated gained and lost regions, stable accessible regions, and synthetic negative controls. Predicted and experimental signals were matched by genomic coordinates and evaluated using correlation, class discrimination, motif-score analysis, and chromosome-grouped cross-validation.

Across the benchmark, AlphaGenome predictions showed moderate rank agreement with experimental signal, with Spearman correlations of 0.606 for young cells and 0.676 for senescent cells. Discrimination was stronger for stable regions and synthetic negatives than for senescence-associated gained and lost regions. Exploratory chromosome-grouped validation showed a modest improvement when the AlphaGenome prediction was added to the young-state experimental signal model, increasing out-of-fold R-squared from 0.290 to 0.316. Motif analysis identified sequence-pattern associations, including an association between TEAD1 motif-score excess and the gained-region class. An exploratory integration of significant RNA-seq results with ATAC-associated genes found substantially higher direction concordance for genes linked exclusively to RS-gained regions; this association persisted in chromosome- and genomic-block-stratified permutation sensitivity analyses.

These findings indicate that AlphaGenome predictions provide useful but limited agreement with experimental regulatory signals in this selected benchmark. They do not establish direct prediction of senescence-associated changes, transcription-factor binding, or causal regulatory mechanisms. Independent validation and state-specific evidence are needed to assess generalizability and biological relevance.


## 1. Research question

How accurately can AlphaGenome predict experimentally observed ATAC-seq regulatory activity in WI-38 fibroblasts, and does agreement differ between genomic regions associated with replicative senescence?

This is a targeted benchmark of sequence-associated regulatory signal. It does not directly test whether AlphaGenome predicts a transition from young to senescent cells.

## 2. Benchmark design

The benchmark contains 10,000 genomic regions, divided into four groups of 2,500:

- **RS-gained:** regions with the largest positive experimental log2 fold changes after filtering atlas peaks by maximum young/senescent signal.
- **RS-lost:** regions with the most negative experimental log2 fold changes after the same signal filter.
- **Stable:** a random sample of accessible atlas peaks with absolute log2 fold change at or below 0.25.
- **Negative:** synthetic genomic controls generated by shuffling stable-region coordinates within chromosomes while excluding the ATAC atlas.

The gained and lost groups are deliberately enriched for extreme experimental changes. The stable group is threshold-selected, and the negative group consists of synthetic controls rather than experimentally confirmed inactive regions. Consequently, the benchmark is not a random or genome-wide representative sample.

## 3. AlphaGenome evaluation

The existing predictions were joined to the experimental ground truth by chromosome, start, and end. The coordinate validation and evaluation scripts reported a one-to-one match for all 10,000 regions.

### Overall signal agreement

| Comparison | Pearson r | Spearman r | MAE | RMSE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Prediction vs young experimental signal | 0.459 | 0.606 | 0.158 | 0.273 |
| Prediction vs senescent experimental signal | 0.545 | 0.676 | 0.125 | 0.217 |

Overall rank correlation was higher for the senescent experimental signal than for the young signal. However, the prediction is a single sequence-derived signal, not a separate prediction for each experimental cell state. This comparison does not establish that AlphaGenome predicts senescence-associated change.

### Agreement within benchmark classes

| Class | Spearman r: prediction vs young | Spearman r: prediction vs senescent |
|---|---:|---:|
| Negative | 0.611 | 0.610 |
| RS-gained | 0.142 | 0.241 |
| RS-lost | 0.356 | 0.321 |
| Stable | 0.521 | 0.508 |

The within-class correlations for gained and lost regions were weaker than those for stable regions and synthetic negatives. These values describe the selected benchmark and should not be interpreted as genome-wide performance.

### Class discrimination

The existing one-vs-rest ranking analysis selected the score direction with the better ROC-AUC for each class.

| Class | Better direction | ROC-AUC |
|---|---|---:|
| RS-gained | Higher predicted signal | 0.550 |
| RS-lost | Higher predicted signal | 0.593 |
| Stable | Higher predicted signal | 0.799 |
| Negative | Lower predicted signal | 0.942 |

AlphaGenome predictions separated the stable and synthetic-negative classes more effectively than the gained and lost classes. Because the negative controls are shuffled outside the atlas and the gained/lost groups were selected using experimental fold changes, these ROC-AUC values must be interpreted in the context of the benchmark construction.

The predicted-signal medians were 0.086 for RS-gained, 0.099 for RS-lost, 0.235 for stable, and 0.010 for negative regions. The gained and lost classes therefore showed only a small difference in their median predicted signals.

An exploratory analysis regressed predicted signal against young experimental signal and compared residuals between gained and lost regions. The residual distributions differed (Cliff's delta = 0.571; Mann–Whitney p = 4.34 × 10^-268). This is a baseline-adjusted association, not a causal test or evidence that the model predicts a cell-state transition.

## 4. Motif analysis

The motif analysis examined TEAD1 and eight additional paper-guided motifs: CEBPA, E2F1, FOS, JUN, NFKB1, RELA, SMAD3, and SMAD4.

Motif score excess was calculated relative to dinucleotide-preserving shuffled sequences. The unified analysis compared gained and lost regions with stable regions, adjusting for sequence length and GC fraction, using HC3 robust standard errors and Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Selected adjusted associations were:

- **TEAD1:** positive score-excess association in RS-gained regions (β = 0.611; adjusted q = 4.44 × 10^-10); no evidence of an association in RS-lost regions.
- **CEBPA:** positive association in RS-gained regions (β = 0.452; q = 8.84 × 10^-7) and negative association in RS-lost regions (β = -0.361; q = 8.90 × 10^-6).
- **FOS:** negative association in RS-gained regions (β = -1.945; q = 1.14 × 10^-15) and positive association in RS-lost regions (β = 1.263; q = 2.05 × 10^-9).
- **E2F1:** negative association in RS-gained regions (β = -0.415; q = 5.10 × 10^-5).
- **RELA:** positive association in RS-gained regions (β = 0.188; q = 0.041).

These are associations between sequence motif scores and selected region classes, not direct evidence of transcription-factor binding or activity. Model R-squared values were low (approximately 0.0005–0.042), so the motif models explain only a small fraction of score-excess variation. Results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, with potential dependence between nearby or overlapping genomic regions also kept in mind.

YAP1 was not treated as a conventional sequence-specific motif in this analysis because it functions primarily as a transcriptional coactivator. A TEAD1 motif association is compatible with investigating YAP/TEAD biology, but it does not demonstrate YAP1 recruitment, activity, or causality. Independent evidence such as expression, pathway, or occupancy data would be needed to test that biological hypothesis.

## 5A. Chromosome-grouped validation of change association

To test whether AlphaGenome predictions add information beyond the young-state experimental ATAC signal, two linear models were evaluated using five-fold cross-validation grouped by chromosome. The target was the experimental log2 fold change between senescent and young WI-38 cells, using the RS-gained, RS-lost, and Stable classes (7,500 regions). Synthetic negative controls were excluded. All reported predictions were out-of-fold.

| Model | Out-of-fold R-squared | Out-of-fold MAE |
|---|---:|---:|
| Young experimental signal only | 0.28986 | 2.0311 |
| Young signal + AlphaGenome prediction | 0.31647 | 1.9791 |
| Change | +0.02661 | 0.0520 lower |

Adding the AlphaGenome prediction modestly improved overall out-of-fold performance on this selected benchmark. The class-wise results were:

| Class | R-squared: young only | R-squared: extended model | MAE: young only | MAE: extended model |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| RS-gained | -14.136 | -13.830 | 2.411 | 2.361 |
| RS-lost | -43.269 | -40.965 | 2.652 | 2.546 |
| Stable | -98.939 | -95.824 | 1.031 | 1.031 |

The class-wise R-squared values remained strongly negative, indicating poor prediction relative to a class-specific mean baseline. In the Stable class, the MAE improvement was negligible despite the numerical R-squared increase. The results therefore do not support a claim of strong within-class prediction.

These analyses are exploratory. The benchmark classes were selected using experimental fold changes, and chromosome-grouped folds do not constitute independent-dataset validation. The modest overall improvement suggests that the AlphaGenome score may provide additional information beyond young-state signal within this benchmark, but does not establish that the model directly predicts senescence-associated regulatory change.

## 5B. ATAC–RNA integration and direction concordance

### Integration and analysis population

Experimental ATAC-region effects were integrated with a batch-adjusted RNA-seq time-course analysis. Associations were retained when the RNA result had an adjusted p-value below 0.05 and the gene identifier mapped unambiguously to a unique gene symbol. Direction concordance was defined by matching the sign of the observed ATAC log2 fold change to the sign of the RNA log2 fold change. Zero-effect associations were excluded from direction-based tests.

The integration produced 3,181 directionally classifiable region–gene associations involving 3,033 distinct ATAC regions and 2,088 unique genes. For the single-class gene sensitivity analysis, genes linked to multiple ATAC classes were excluded. The resulting analysis included 1,675 genes: 347 Negative, 171 RS-gained, 474 RS-lost, and 683 Stable. Each gene contributed one binary direction-concordance outcome.

### Single-class gene concordance

| ATAC class | Genes | Concordant | Concordance | 95% Wilson confidence interval |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Negative | 347 | 192 | 55.33% | 50.07–60.48% |
| RS-gained | 171 | 143 | 83.63% | 77.35–88.42% |
| RS-lost | 474 | 273 | 57.59% | 53.10–61.97% |
| Stable | 683 | 342 | 50.07% | 46.33–53.81% |

The RS-gained group showed a 29.97-percentage-point higher concordance rate than all other classes combined (83.63% versus 53.66%). Exploratory Fisher exact comparisons also found higher odds of concordance for RS-gained genes than for Negative, RS-lost, and Stable genes. These comparisons are based on the same selected dataset and should not be considered independent replications.

### Chromosome-stratified permutation sensitivity analysis

A chromosome-stratified permutation test preserved the observed class counts within each chromosome while randomizing class assignments. The observed concordance outcomes were held fixed. Across 20,000 permutations, the RS-gained concordance advantage remained pronounced relative to Negative, RS-lost, Stable, and all other classes combined. The empirical one-sided p-value was 0.00005 for each comparison after the add-one correction; this is the minimum reportable p-value with 20,000 permutations, rather than an estimate of the exact underlying probability.

### Spatial-block permutation sensitivity analysis

To assess whether broad genomic location alone could explain the association, a second analysis shuffled class labels within chromosome-specific 5 Mb and 10 Mb blocks. Each gene was assigned to a block using the midpoint of its full annotation span; the full span was used where multiple annotation intervals existed for the same gene. Each permutation preserved class counts within each block and retained the observed gene-level concordance outcomes.

| Comparison | Observed difference | 5 Mb BH-adjusted p-value | 10 Mb BH-adjusted p-value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RS-gained vs Negative | +28.29 percentage points | 0.00310 | 0.00005 |
| RS-gained vs RS-lost | +26.03 percentage points | 0.000067 | 0.00005 |
| RS-gained vs Stable | +33.55 percentage points | 0.000067 | 0.00005 |
| RS-gained vs all other classes | +29.97 percentage points | 0.000067 | 0.00005 |

The four p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure separately within each block-size analysis. Empirical p-values used 20,000 permutations and an add-one correction. Values of 0.00005 represent the permutation-resolution floor, not exact probabilities.

The persistence of the RS-gained association under these chromosome- and block-stratified procedures suggests that broad genomic location and class composition alone do not readily explain the observed difference. However, these tests do not eliminate fine-scale spatial dependence, shared regulatory elements, gene-network relationships, or other sources of biological dependence. The two block sizes and chromosome-stratified analysis are sensitivity analyses of the same data, not independent validation.

### Interpretation and limitations

The results indicate a strong association between membership in the RS-gained class and agreement between ATAC and RNA effect directions among genes linked exclusively to one ATAC class. They do not demonstrate that ATAC changes cause expression changes, that every linked region regulates its associated gene, or that the result generalizes to independent datasets. The RNA coefficient represents a batch-adjusted continuous time-course association, not a direct young-versus-senescent contrast. Significance-based RNA selection, the benchmark construction, gene-to-region mapping, and possible dependence among observations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

## 6. Limitations

1. The benchmark uses selected experimental extremes, threshold-selected stable peaks, and synthetic negative controls; it is not representative of all genomic regions.
2. The AlphaGenome ATAC signal uses an IMR-90 fetal lung fibroblast proxy, not a senescence-specific WI-38 model.
3. A single sequence-derived prediction cannot directly represent the difference between young and senescent experimental states.
4. Correlation and class discrimination do not establish causal regulation or transcription-factor binding.
5. Motif associations have low explanatory power and may be affected by genomic-region dependence, sequence composition, and benchmark selection.
6. The analyses described here do not constitute independent validation on a separate dataset.

## 7. Conclusion

In this selected WI-38 replicative-senescence benchmark, AlphaGenome predictions showed moderate overall rank agreement with experimental ATAC-seq signal, with higher agreement against the senescent group mean than against the young group mean. Discrimination was strongest for stable regions and synthetic negative controls, while separation of gained and lost regions was weak. Motif-score analysis identified several class-associated sequence patterns, including a TEAD1 association with gained regions, that may motivate further biological investigation. The exploratory ATAC–RNA integration additionally found higher direction concordance among genes linked exclusively to RS-gained regions, a pattern that persisted under chromosome- and spatial-block-stratified permutation sensitivity analyses. This is an association within the selected dataset, not evidence of causal regulation or independent validation. Overall, the findings support a targeted evaluation of the strengths and limitations of sequence-based regulatory prediction; they do not show that AlphaGenome directly predicts senescence or establish a causal YAP1/TEAD mechanism.
